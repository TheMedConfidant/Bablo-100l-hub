Experiment 3 — Calibration of pipette, burette and thermometer

A. Calibration of pipette and burette

Source procedure: Weigh a clean, dry specimen tube. Deliver a stated volume of distilled water (the manual specifies 25 or 10 cm³) using the burette and determine the mass of water by difference. Repeat five times, then repeat using the matching pipette.

Calculations

  1. Mass of water: m = (mass of tube + water) − (mass of empty tube).
  2. Mean mass: m̄ = Σm / n.
  3. Using water density at the measured temperature, calculate delivered volume: V = m̄ / ρ (ensure units match; for ρ in g cm⁻³, V is cm³).
  4. Absolute volume error: ΔV = Vmeasured − Vnominal. Relative error: |ΔV| / Vnominal; percentage error is this value × 100%.
  5. Standard deviation: s = √[Σ(mᵢ − m̄)²/(n−1)]. Standard error of the mean: s/√n.

Conclusion: Compare the absolute relative errors and repeatability of the pipette and burette. The smaller error indicates better accuracy; lower spread indicates better precision. Use your own readings.

B. Calibration of a thermometer

The manual specifies measuring the thermometer in an ice–water mixture and in steam above boiling water, allowing time to equilibrate at each point. Record the two readings and compare with the expected fixed points for the local pressure. Discuss zero error, immersion depth, equilibration and parallax as possible systematic errors.

No observed mass or thermometer readings are supplied in the scan, so a numerical result cannot be calculated from the source alone.

Experiment 4 — Graph plotting and errors of a fitted line

The manual gives a table of paired x and y observations and asks for a graph. Plot the measured pairs, label both axes with quantities and units, use a sensible scale, and draw a best-fit line rather than joining points one by one.

For a straight-line fit y = mx + c, the gradient is m = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁) using two well-separated points on the fitted line; the intercept c is where the line crosses the y-axis. Do not use two raw data points unless they lie on the fitted line.

The scan gives the approximate scatter estimate Sₘ = 2w/(nR) for 3 < n < 12, where n is the number of points, w is the range of vertical scatter, and R is the total x-coordinate range. Estimate uncertainty from the graph and state units. Check the original manual image for the exact symbol formatting before submission.

Answer guide: Submit the graph, gradient with units, intercept with units, and a short statement describing the trend and uncertainty.

Experiment 5 — Theory of indicators

Core principle from the manual: An acid–base indicator is a weak organic acid or base whose ionized and unionized forms have different colours. The colour depends on their ratio and therefore on pH. The endpoint is the observed colour change; it should be as close as possible to the equivalence point.

HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻

For the indicator equilibrium, the transition range is around its pKₐ (approximately pKₐ ± 1 pH unit). Choose an indicator whose colour-transition range overlaps the steep pH change around the equivalence point. The manual notes that an indicator’s neutral colour does not necessarily correspond to pH 7.

Answer guide

  • Explain endpoint versus equivalence point: observed indicator change versus stoichiometric completion.
  • Explain why indicator choice depends on the acid/base strengths and the shape of the titration curve.
  • When asked for indicator colours, state the indicator, acidic colour and alkaline colour accurately for the indicator used.
Experiment 6 — Strong acid against strong base

Aim: Titrate a strong acid against a strong base. The manual specifies 0.2 M NaOH, nitric acid of unknown concentration, phenolphthalein, a 50 cm³ burette, 25 cm³ pipette and conical flask. It instructs titration to the pink-to-colourless endpoint when acid is in the burette, repeating until concordant titres are obtained.

Balanced equation: HNO₃ + NaOH → NaNO₃ + H₂O (1:1 stoichiometry).

  1. Average concordant titre: average the titres that agree within the manual’s stated tolerance.
  2. Moles NaOH in flask: n = C × V(dm³). For 25.00 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ NaOH, n = 0.200 × 0.02500 = 0.00500 mol.
  3. At equivalence, 1 mol HNO₃ reacts with 1 mol NaOH, so moles HNO₃ = moles NaOH.
  4. Acid concentration: C(HNO₃) = n(HNO₃) / Vtitre(dm³).
  5. Mass concentration: g dm⁻³ = mol dm⁻³ × Mᵣ; Mᵣ(HNO₃) ≈ 63.01 g mol⁻¹.

Two other possible indicators: methyl orange and bromothymol blue are suitable candidates for a strong acid–strong base titration, depending on the endpoint requirements.

A final numeric HNO₃ concentration requires your actual concordant titre; it is not present in the scanned handout.

Experiment 7 — Weak acid against strong base

Aim: Determine a suitable indicator for a weak acid versus a strong base. The manual specifies 0.1 M acetic acid (CH₃COOH), 0.1 M NaOH and phenolphthalein.

Equation: CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O (1:1 stoichiometry).

  1. Calculate moles of acetic acid delivered from the burette: n(acid) = 0.100 × Vacid(dm³).
  2. At equivalence, moles NaOH = moles acetic acid.
  3. Calculate NaOH concentration: C(NaOH) = n(NaOH) / VNaOH(dm³).
  4. Convert to mass concentration: g dm⁻³ = C × 40.00 for NaOH.

Indicator reasoning: The equivalence solution is basic because acetate hydrolyses in water; phenolphthalein is suitable because its transition occurs in the alkaline region. Use actual delivered volumes for the final answer.

Experiment 8 — Determine pKₐ of a weak acid using a pH meter

The manual uses the Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship:

pH = pKₐ + log₁₀([salt]/[acid])

It specifies preparing seven mixtures from a weak acid and its sodium salt, measuring each pH with a pH meter, and calculating pKₐ.

Rearrange: pKₐ = pH − log₁₀([salt]/[acid]). If both stock solutions have the same concentration, the concentration ratio equals the mixed-volume ratio, provided dilution and preparation follow the manual.

For each mixture: calculate the salt-to-acid ratio, substitute the measured pH, and calculate pKₐ. Then calculate the mean pK̄ₐ = ΣpKₐ/n, sample standard deviation s = √[Σ(pKₐᵢ−pK̄ₐ)²/(n−1)], and standard error SE = s/√n. The scan’s mixture table is reproduced as volume guidance: salt solution 10 cm³ in each; weak-acid volumes are blank for mixtures 1–3 and 10, 20, 40, 80 cm³ for mixtures 4–7; the corresponding diluted weak-acid volumes for mixtures 1–3 are 12, 25, 50 cm³; distilled-water volumes are 78, 65, 40, 80, 70, 50, 10 cm³ respectively. Confirm this table against the original page before lab preparation.

Measured pH values are required to calculate numerical pKₐ results.

Experiment 9 — Determine titration curves using a pH meter

Source procedure summary: Standardize the pH meter with buffer solutions (the manual names pH 4 and pH 7). Pipette 25.0 cm³ of 0.1 M HCl into a beaker, add distilled water to cover the electrode, record initial pH, then add measured portions of 0.1 M NaOH while stirring and record pH after each addition. Use smaller additions near the endpoint and continue beyond it as specified in the handout.

  1. Make a table of cumulative NaOH volume added (cm³) and measured pH.
  2. Plot pH (y-axis) against NaOH volume (x-axis).
  3. Identify the equivalence region from the sharpest pH rise; for strong acid–strong base it is near pH 7 at 25°C.
  4. Compare the graphical endpoint with the indicator endpoint. The manual asks whether methyl orange gives an endpoint at pH 7; discuss the indicator transition range rather than assuming it does.

Neutralization: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. Theoretical starting acid amount is 0.100 mol dm⁻³ × 0.0250 dm³ = 0.00250 mol; at equivalence, the same amount of NaOH has been added, assuming stated concentrations and no losses.

Experiment 10 — Redox titrations

Principle: Redox titrations involve electron transfer between titrant and analyte. The manual covers permanganate titration and iodine titration. Potassium permanganate can act as a self-indicator; starch is used for iodine titration and forms a blue complex with iodine.

A. Permanganate titration

The handout prepares ferrous ammonium sulphate solution and potassium permanganate solution, then standardizes the KMnO₄ against 25 cm³ aliquots of the iron(II) solution in dilute sulfuric acid. Endpoint: a permanent faint pink colour.

Balanced ionic equation in acidic solution: MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O.

Find moles Fe²⁺ from the accurately weighed salt and final solution volume (use the correct formula and molar mass printed on the reagent bottle). From the equation, n(MnO₄⁻) = n(Fe²⁺)/5. Then C(KMnO₄) = n(MnO₄⁻)/Vtitre(dm³). Sulfuric acid supplies the acidic medium needed for permanganate to be reduced to Mn²⁺.

B. Iodine / iodometric titration

The source gives IO₃⁻ + 5I⁻ + 6H⁺ → 3I₂ + 3H₂O and I₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2I⁻. Iodine liberated from iodate/iodide in acid is titrated with sodium thiosulfate. Add starch near the pale-yellow stage, then continue until the blue colour just disappears.

Net titration equation: I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻. From the iodate equation, 1 mol IO₃⁻ produces 3 mol I₂; each mole I₂ consumes 2 mol thiosulfate, so 1 mol IO₃⁻ corresponds to 6 mol S₂O₃²⁻. Calculate thiosulfate concentration from the known KIO₃ amount, the 10.0 cm³ aliquot, and the measured thiosulfate titre.

Exact concentrations require the recorded mass and concordant titre values from the actual experiment.
